The god you inherited

I come from a place where God is truth and religion is tradition. Not necessarily because one has been discovered and the other invented, but because one is inherited through the other. Before I was old enough to ask what God was, I had already been given an answer. Before I could distinguish faith from certainty, I had already been taught what was sacred.

I look around and see rituals performed with such devotion that questioning the ritual itself can feel almost like an act of violence. I see food offered to the divine and then discarded while someone somewhere goes to sleep hungry. I see people stop their busy lives when a cow or a bull enters the road, careful not to disturb it, while hardly anyone stops to question why the animal has been left to wander through the chaos of a crowded city.

Sometimes I cannot escape a strange coincidence: the religion we call truth so often seems to be the religion we were born closest to.

A child born in one house learns that Krishna is real. Another learns that Christ is real. Another learns that Allah is real. Another is taught the Buddha's way. Another grows up without any home to teach him. And before any of these children are old enough to understand what a metaphysical claim even is, they have already inherited an answer to one of the oldest questions humanity has ever asked. Then we grow older and call it faith.

But how much of that faith was ever chosen?

The stories of Krishna and Ram were not merely stories I encountered in a book. They entered my life before I had the ability to distinguish mythology from history, tradition from evidence. They arrived with my first lessons about the world, alongside family, language, morality, and the things I was told were sacred. And I do not blame my parents for this. They inherited their gods too.

That is what makes the whole thing more difficult.

When a son tells his mother, “I don't believe in your God,” she may feel that he has rejected something sacred. But I sometimes wonder whether she has ever considered the reverse. Somewhere in another country, another mother may be praying just as desperately for her son to return safely from war, except she is praying to a different God. Can one mother look at the other and say, “Your God is not the truth. Your prayers are only stories. Your blessings do not work”?

If she can, then what exactly separates her certainty from the certainty of the other? And if both mothers are sincere, both are terrified, both love their children, both pray with everything they have. Does sincerity make either God more real?

I don't know. That “I don't know” bothers me more than I would like to admit.

Human beings have always been remarkably good at building explanations for the things they cannot bear to leave unexplained. We construct gods, systems, doctrines, philosophies, morals. We give the universe a voice because silence frightens us.

But then I have to stop myself. Because what if I am doing the same thing?

What if I am criticizing people for inheriting certainty while quietly inheriting my own certainty from somewhere else? What if my suspicion of religion has been shaped by the same world that shaped their devotion? What if I am not free of conditioning merely because I have rejected the beliefs I was conditioned to accept?

That question is harder to live with. The deepest illusion may be believing that you are the one who has escaped illusion. We are not the centre of the universe. We do not have all the answers.

Why?

Why should I believe something simply because my society has repeated it for centuries?

Why should I reject it simply because I have grown suspicious of it?

Perhaps religion is false. Perhaps some religion is true. Perhaps I have not yet understood the question well enough to know what “true” would even mean in this context. Perhaps there is a God. Perhaps there is none. Perhaps reality is stranger than either possibility allows me to imagine.

People hold on to these things because they are human. Because their dead are buried beneath them. Because their mothers prayed beside them. Because their fathers taught them stories they themselves received from their fathers. Because entire civilizations have been built around these beliefs. To ask someone to abandon their religion is sometimes to ask to lose some of themself.

No one is free from this enigma, for it is bitter to all to ask: If I had been born somewhere else, who would I have become? And if I cannot know who I would have become, how much of the person asking this question is also an accident? I do not know what the answer is for myself. For once, I think I would rather live honestly with that uncertainty than manufacture a certainty merely because uncertainty frightens me.
